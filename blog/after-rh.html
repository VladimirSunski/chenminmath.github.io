Written after the advent of a Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

“You have received a better and more complete education than the others, and you are more capable of sharing both ways of life. Down you must go then, each in his turn, to the habitation of the others and accustom yourselves to the observation of the obscure things there. For once habituated you will discern them infinitely better than the dwellers there, and you will know what each of the ‘idols’ is and whereof it is a semblance, because you have seen the reality of the beautiful, the just and the good.
——-Plato, Republic, Book VII, 520c–d

In the future, the year 2026 will be remembered as a miracle year in the history of mathematics. Friday afternoon (November 20), breaking news kept popping up on my smartphone: Apart from a complete construction of the blow-up solution of the Navier-Stokes equations, Alpöge, a mathematician working at Anthropic announced the discovery of the proof1 of the Riemann hypothesis (RH) with Claude. Unlike several big releases that we all experienced in the recent months, only a compressed archive containing more-than 17 million Lean code was made public. According to the releaser, the unreleased natural-language proof draft exceeds 250,000 pages, which remains beyond his current understanding. Thanks to independent audit of the Lean-codes by different groups, the proof is judged correct.

Despite the numbing volumes of news of conjectures solved by AI, this discovery of a proof of RH (and also GRH) is a landmark event. For generations of mathematical researchers and students, RH has been a lodestar. On the one hand, the answer to RH reveals deep patterns in the distribution of prime numbers, a central object of mathematics. On the other hand, and more remarkably, the way of discovering this 167-year-old dusty proof is unprecedented: the proof, mainly discovered by a superintelligent machine, exceeds the limits of human readability for now. How long will it take for the mathematical community to entirely unpack the proof?

With the mathematical community experiencing a period of chaotic transition, broad and deep discussion within it has become a daily topic during the coffee and lunch time. Whether one is optimistic or pessimistic, a basic consensus is that mathematics is undergoing industrialisation. People tend to take this concept for granted by drawing an analogy with the Industrial Revolution. Rather, I would like to analyze carefully this analogy, in order to articulate a perspective of a benign new steady state for the future of human mathematical activity. Indeed, current frontier LLMs have crossed the threshold of superhuman problem-solving capacity; no individual mathematician can ignore their existence and still cultivate their own garden in peace. Moreover, the way that the proof of RH was released poses a serious problem of overcapacity in the industrialisation procedure.

According to the definition on Wikipedia, industrialisation is "the period of social and economic change that transforms a human group from an agrarian and feudal society into an industrial society." The core closed loop of industrialisation consists of the input end (raw materials and factors of production), the transformation end (automated productive capacity), and the output and consumption end (scaled products and the consumer market). Drawing a one-to-one correspondence with the mathematical world, the ingredient is the thousand-year-old human literature that has built the current mathematical cathedral; frontier LLMs are automated machines for producing new theorems, proofs, and theories as products at scale. On reflection, it turns out that the consumption market is now a major obstruction to the closed loop of industrialisation, rooted in what Terence Tao has repeatedly called proof indigestion.

Let me restrict the discussion here to the domain of pure mathematics. I regard pure mathematics as a domain of human activity, driven by the pursuit of mathematical understanding, intellectual challenge and aesthetic beauty. Traditionally, the pure math community is a relatively closed system. The handicraft mode of mathematical research has been very stable for centuries . The reward structure of this system is monolithic. Its products include theorems, proofs, concepts and new theories, with particular academic credit attached to difficult problem-solving. The value of these products is epistemic and aesthetic, and the single, immediate consumption market is the community itself. With the superhuman problem-solving power of the machine, there is now a growing impedance mismatch between the production of theorems and proofs and human understanding; the problem of overcapacity prevents the closed loop of industrialisation from closing in this old system.

Suppose researchers with expertise in mathematical domain A wants to drawn on the output of domain B 2 (B might equal A); their demand is usually based on two reasons: the motivation of understanding ideas and tools in order to apply them to their own research; the aesthetic pleasure of understanding a beautiful theorem. For an individual researcher, however, due to the hard constraints of human attention, the probability of newly generated theorems receiving serious attention decreases as the volume of results going beyond the reader’s capacity to absorb them, even though AI tools could significantly improve reading efficiency. In this sense, the expected marginal benefit of additional proof production to an individual reader’s understanding may diminish, leaving many new results beyond the reach of individual readers.

Obviously, this argument does not automatically extend to the collective level of understanding: more researchers with powerful AI tools might absorb more mathematical knowledge. Yet, the collective capacity to select, evaluate, connect and assimilate new results does not necessarily grow at the same pace as proof production. These processes still require intellectual judgment and substantial efforts to understand them. Besides, industrialisation enables standardized production at scale, but the value of theorems in pure mathematics is uneven and hard to measure. If the mathematical community cannot find reasonable ways to evaluate new theorems and theories and allocate resources to develop a deeper understanding of them, the industrialisation process will be unsustainable for human mathematical activity.

The discussion above is not another Luddite argument against industrialisation. The underlying assumption is that the pure-math community is a closed system with a simple supply-and-demand relationship. In the past, the reward system was built on proof scarcity: problem-solving was extremely difficult and highly valued. Now, encouraging the extensive deployment of powerful proof-production machines to accelerate the research process—by merely proving more significant theorems, without changing the old system—would not be a successful route to industrialisation. From what I have observed, some mathematicians are waiting to see the limits of these frontier LLMs’ capabilities, while many others, whether by choice or under pressure, are engaging in lottery-style problem-solving. The concern is its becoming a new routine research mode within the unchanged reward system. The immediate consequences are clear: lack of oversight over the misuse of closed-source LLMs; the proliferation of papers threatening to overwhelm the peer-review system; the depletion of the old problem pool without comparable efforts to replenish it by formulating new ones.

Unlike pure mathematics, the rapid development of artificial intelligence offers a more clearly optimistic outlook for applied mathematics. In these areas, the products can be any mathematical outputs embedded in industrial products, drawn from engineering and the interdisciplinary sciences. So demand is by definition diverse: an encryption algorithm in finance, a convergence proof for a numerical scheme in fluid mechanics, medical imaging software, …In these areas linking theoretical mathematics with engineering, the extensive deployment of AI tools can decrease the cost of modelling, computation and theoretical verification. Correspondingly, demand for applied mathematics will increase substantially as AI tools become more powerful and more affordable. In applied mathematics, though, obtaining reliable results matters more than the slow pace of human understanding; yet the rapid development of applied mathematics will in turn bring new theoretical problems to pure mathematics. Therefore, breaking the barrier between pure and applied mathematics opens a path to successful industrialisation.

Even for pure mathematics, which focuses on human understanding, with reasonable reform of the system, the core human value of mathematical activity would survive the shock wave of industrialisation. Rather than deciding whether to embrace AI, the key is to change the current closed state of the system, expanding the diverse output end and enlarging the consumption market. For example, teaching activities have been undervalued. For a long time, teaching provides much of the financial support for research mathematics, while the highly competitive reward systems of universities and famous institutes are designed for excellent researchers. For academics, teaching and research reinforce each other. However, the publish-or-perish rule and the intense arms race of publication force them to compress their other service. Research outputs on top journals are already too complicated to be communicated across experts from different domains, let alone taught in a master’s course. The debt of understanding are already there, regardless of AI.

With powerful LLMs as tools, the output end could be rich and diverse, not just vast quantities of unreadable raw proofs of conjectures: personalizing cross-disciplinary learning materials, distilling theoretical problems from applied mathematics, reforming syllabi and advanced mathematics education, writing expositions of important results for peers and students, writing high-quality popular mathematics articles,.... Many of these activities are side work of a relatively small group of mathematicians in the past time. In the future, such work could be extended to more mathematicians, so as to serve the cultural needs of a growing number of amateurs and engineers. To be honest, I myself have not been doing well in the past. But from now on, it seems that I have more work to do.

167 years after Riemann had written: "...,und es ist sehr wahrscheinlich, dass alle Wurzeln reell sind", the search for a proof that he had left aside, together with the transformation of mathematics, has arrived in a way he could never have imagined.
